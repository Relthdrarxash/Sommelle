Why Sommelle works the way it does

The app is deliberately simple: one small action a day, little text. This page explains the rest. For each step, you will find what Sommelle does, why, and what it is based on. When a rule is our own choice rather than a study result, we say so.

Health professionals will find the details of the calculations and thresholds in the scientific dossier.

The principles

  • The first-line method. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the treatment advised before sleeping pills by European and American guidelines. Sommelle follows its steps, without inventing new ones.
  • Finishing matters more than anything. Digital versions of CBT-I work, but many people stop along the way. So Sommelle is designed to be sustainable: an entry of about twenty seconds, and a forgotten night that can be filled in later.
  • Nothing to make you feel guilty. No daily score, no streak to keep up, no red. A bad week is information for adjusting your schedule, not a failure. This is our own choice.
  • No numbers at night. Checking the time when you can't sleep keeps insomnia going. Sommelle never asks you for exact minutes and shows no time at night.
  • You keep the choice. The most demanding part of the program, the sleep window, is explained before you start, and you can follow the program without it.

The morning diary

Each morning, you log your night: bedtime, time to fall asleep, awakenings, rise time, and how it felt. Durations are chosen from simple estimates, such as "15 min" or "30 min".

Why. The diary is the basis of all CBT-I: it is what allows your schedule to be adapted to your real nights. Sommelle uses the reference version used by researchers. Estimates are enough: asking for exact minutes would push you to check the time at night.

The sleep window

After an observation week, Sommelle suggests a bedtime and a rise time. At first, the time spent in bed roughly matches the time you actually sleep, without going below 5 hours 30 minutes. You choose the rise time, the same every day, weekends included; the bedtime follows from it.

Each week, Sommelle compares the time slept with the time spent in bed. If you sleep most of the time you are lying down, the window grows by 15 minutes. If you often lie awake in bed, it shrinks by 15 minutes, never below the minimum. In between, it stays the same.

Why. Spending a long time in bed without sleeping fragments sleep and links the bed with being awake. Reducing this time for a while makes sleep deeper and more continuous; it is then lengthened as sleep consolidates. It is one of the most effective parts of CBT-I, and the one that takes the most effort.

The precise thresholds (85% and 80% of time in bed spent asleep, 75% and 80% from age 65), the minimum of 5 hours 30 minutes and the maximum of 9 hours are reasoned choices, which we have reviewed by sleep professionals.

What to expect in the first weeks

People who gave up on the sleep window along the way often explain that they did not know how long it would last, or that they feared its effects. Those who had been told what to expect felt ready. So here is what lies ahead.

  • More tiredness at first. In the first weeks, daytime sleepiness often increases. This is expected and temporary. If you feel drowsy, do not drive or operate dangerous machinery.
  • A window that then grows. As soon as you sleep most of the time you spend in bed, you go to bed a quarter of an hour earlier each week. The short window is not a lasting state.
  • A six- to eight-week program. Afterwards, you can keep up the diary if you wish, and the window keeps adjusting.
  • If it is too hard, talk to your doctor. The program without the sleep window is still useful, with a more gradual effect.

When you can't sleep

An "I can't sleep" button opens a dark screen, with no time or numbers, that reminds you what to do: get up, go to another room, do something calm in soft light, and go back to bed when sleep comes. The screen closes by itself.

Why. Lying in bed waiting for sleep pushes it away: the bed ends up being linked with wakefulness and tension. Getting up helps link it with sleep again. This instruction has existed since the 1970s and is part of every guideline.

The exercises

From the second week, an exercise of about five minutes unlocks each week. Each one uses a classic CBT-I technique:

  • Ideas about sleep: common beliefs, such as "I need 8 hours", that increase tension at bedtime, and a more accurate version of each.
  • Putting your day to rest: writing down your worries early in the evening so you don't take them to bed.
  • Stop chasing sleep: the harder you try to fall asleep, the more awake you stay; the exercise takes that pressure away.
  • Back to the most likely: a few questions about what you fear for the next day and what actually happened the other times.
  • Relaxing the body: tensing then relaxing your muscles, in the evening.

The choice of these five exercises and their order are our own choices.

Why Sommelle sometimes refers you to a doctor

The starting questionnaire spots situations where the sleep window would be risky or unsuitable: snoring with pauses in breathing, falling asleep during the day, bipolar disorder, epilepsy, pregnancy, night work, nights interrupted by someone else. Depending on the case, Sommelle does not reduce your time in bed, or advises you to talk to a doctor.

Why. Reducing time in bed causes tiredness at first, which can be dangerous at the wheel or destabilize some illnesses. And some conditions that look like insomnia, such as sleep apnea, are treated differently.

What Sommelle does not do

  • It does not make a diagnosis and does not replace medical advice.
  • It does not guide you off sleeping pills, which is done with your doctor.
  • Its own effectiveness has not been evaluated in a clinical study: it is the methods it uses that have been.
